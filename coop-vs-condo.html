Co-op or Condo? How to Choose in New York

Ownership, board approval, down payment, monthly costs and subletting. The real differences, and which one fits how you plan to live.

By Olof Hermelin  ·  Douglas Elliman  ·  5 min read

Most first-time buyers in New York hit this question in the first week. The two can look the same from the hallway, but they are different things to own. Here are the differences that matter.

What you actually own

In a condo, you own your apartment as real property and you get a deed. In a co-op, you own shares in the corporation that owns the building, and a lease that gives you the right to live in your apartment. Most of the older apartment buildings in Manhattan are co-ops.

Getting approved

A co-op board reviews your finances in detail, interviews you, and can turn you down without giving a reason, as long as it does not discriminate. A condo board usually has only a right of first refusal, which is rarely used. If you want a simpler path, or your income is hard to document, a condo is easier.

Down payment and financing

Co-ops set their own rules. Many ask for at least 20% to 25% down, some ask for more, and a few allow no financing at all. Many boards also want to see savings left over after closing, often enough to cover one to two years of your housing costs. Condos follow the lender’s rules, so you can often put down less.

Monthly costs

A co-op charges one monthly maintenance fee. It covers building staff and upkeep, the building’s property taxes, and any mortgage on the building itself. A condo charges common charges, and you pay your property taxes separately. Compare the totals, not the labels.

Price and closing costs

For the same size and location, a co-op usually costs less than a condo. Co-ops also cost less to close, because there is no mortgage recording tax and no title insurance.

Renting it out

This is the big one for investors. Most co-ops limit subletting: for example, only after you have lived there for a set time, only for a few years, and only with board approval. Condos are far more flexible. If you may rent the apartment out one day, a condo is usually the safer choice.

And a condop?

You will see this word in listings. In everyday use, it means a co-op that runs with condo-style rules: lighter approval and easier subletting. Ask for the building’s actual rules before you rely on the label.

The Bottom Line

Choose a co-op for more space at a lower price in a building of owner-residents. Choose a condo for flexibility, easier financing and the freedom to rent it out.
